# The Neighborhood

The daily-life radius around the Williams apartment — the deli, the dry cleaner, the newsstand, the corner where Danny hails cabs.

Another **Tardis directory** — the neighborhood isn't mapped in detail because it doesn't need to be. It exists as a pool of minor encounters: the doorman's wife who needs Copa tickets, the dry cleaner who shrunk Danny's suit, the newsstand owner who has an opinion about everything. These interactions are the texture of urban life, the small social transactions that a New York sitcom uses the way Mayberry uses Main Street.

The difference: Main Street is a fixed cast. The neighborhood is a rotating one. People come through and may never appear again. This isn't loneliness — it's New York's version of community, where repetition of encounters gradually builds familiarity without the permanence Mayberry assumes.

The neighborhood is the **centrifugal force** made geographic. It constantly introduces new stimuli. The apartment is the centripetal counterweight that absorbs them.
